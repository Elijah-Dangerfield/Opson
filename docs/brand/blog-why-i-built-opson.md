# Blog draft: why I built Opson

Headline: The number I got wrong at fourteen

Other headlines to consider:

- Why I built a calorie tracker that lets you hide the calories
- I counted calories at fourteen. This is the app I wish I'd had.

SEO description: Opson's founder on counting calories as a teenager, the
mistake that started a binge and restrict cycle, and why his tracker lets you
choose how many numbers you see.

## The post

I was fourteen the first time a number on a screen decided how my day had
gone.

It was the number at the bottom of my diary in MyFitnessPal, the one that told
me how many calories I had left. If it was still positive at the end of the
night, I'd had a good day. I remember the small, clean feeling of closing the
app with room to spare. I also remember going to bed hungry, and telling myself
that was what a good day felt like.

I didn't understand what that number was. Nobody had explained it to me, and I
didn't think to ask.

## The mistake

There are two numbers that sound alike and mean very different things. Your
basal metabolic rate, or BMR, is roughly what your body burns lying still all
day, just to keep you alive. Your total daily energy expenditure is that plus
everything else: walking to class, playing sports, thinking, and at fourteen,
growing.

I had read somewhere that to lose weight you eat 500 calories less than you
burn. So I found a number for what I burned and subtracted. The number I found
was my BMR. I treated it as my whole day.

That put me at about 1,000 calories a day, which is far less than a growing kid
needs. I didn't know it was a mistake. I thought it was discipline.

## The cycle

On the days I hit that number, I felt great, proud and in control. I was
also starving, and you can only be starving for so long.

Sooner or later I'd go over. Maybe it was a birthday, or a long day at school,
or nothing at all. And once I'd gone over, the day was ruined in my head. There
was no point being careful anymore, because I wasn't going to hit the number
anyway. So I wasn't careful at all. I'd eat whatever was around until I felt
sick, then promise myself that tomorrow I'd be perfect.

Tomorrow I would try to be perfect, and the whole thing started again.

People call this a binge and restrict cycle. I didn't have a name for it then.
I just knew there were two kinds of days, good ones and failed ones, and that I
had a lot more failed ones than I wanted. There was no middle. I was either on
the wagon or completely off it.

What it did to me over time was quieter than any single bad day. Food stopped
being food and became a test I kept taking. My body became something I was
managing, mostly badly. That relationship took years to change.

## What changed

I wish I had a cleaner story here. Mostly it was time, getting older, and
slowly learning what the numbers actually meant and what they didn't. At some
point a big day stopped meaning I had to make up for it, and a missed number
stopped meaning I'd failed.

Today I'm fine seeing all my calories. I track them, and I like having the
information. That matters to me, because the answer to what happened to me
isn't to hide numbers from everyone. Lots of people use them well. Some people
need them for medical reasons. They should get every number they want.

But I keep thinking about that fourteen-year-old, and how little it would have
taken to point him somewhere else.

## What Opson does differently

Opson is a food log. You can use it like any other calorie counter, with every
number on every screen. You can also choose to see fewer. There are four modes:
All numbers, Macros with grams, Macros only, and No numbers. Whatever you pick
holds everywhere in the app, so a calorie count doesn't sneak back in while
you're adding a food.

Goals are a range by default, not one number. When I was fourteen, a single
target meant every day was a pass or a fail. A range has room in it. And when
you go past the top of it, nothing turns red, and the app never shows a negative
number of calories left.

When you wrap up a big day, Opson tells you that everyone has big days and you
don't need to make up for this one. It explains why, too: eating a lot less
after a big day tends to lead to more overeating later. That's the sentence I
needed. What kept my cycle going was always the plan to make up for a big day
the next morning.

You can mark any day as untracked. A holiday, a bad week, a day you just don't
want to look. Trends skips it. If you've turned on the app's run of days, it
waits for you instead of breaking. That run counts the days you showed up and
logged something, never how little you ate, and it forgives a quiet day. If you
don't want it, one tap turns the celebrations off.

When you set up the app, it asks whether numbers or tracking have been hard for
you before. If you say yes, it won't hand you a goal number at all, and
celebrations stay gentle.

And there's a page called Find support that lists eating disorder helplines for
your country. If you set a calorie goal lower than most people should go without
a doctor, Opson tells you so and links there. It doesn't lock you out. I
remember how I would have reacted to being told no: I'd have found a different
app, or done it on paper. A note that treats you like a person and shows you
where help is seemed more likely to land.

I want to go further. My goal is for Opson to notice when someone's log starts
to look like mine did, and gently point them toward help. That has to be done
carefully, with people who know more about eating disorders than I do, and I'd
rather get it right than ship it fast.

## Who it's for

I want this to be the friendliest tracker there is, for anyone who opens it.
Someone counting every gram before a competition should get every gram. Someone
in recovery whose dietitian asked them to log meals without looking at numbers
should get that instead, and neither of them should feel like they're using the
lesser version.

If you're fourteen and reading this, you probably don't need a calorie tracker
at all, mine included. I'd tell you what I wish someone had told me: the number
you're aiming for is almost certainly too low, and a day you go over is not a
day you failed.

I don't know if Opson would have saved me from those years. I might have found
a way to turn any app into a test. But I would have had a choice about which
numbers I saw, and on the worst days, something on the screen telling me
tomorrow could be ordinary.

*If food or eating feels hard right now, you don't have to work it out alone.
In the US, the ANAD Helpline is 1-888-375-7767 and the National Alliance for
Eating Disorders is 1-866-662-1235. In the UK, Beat's helpline is
0808 801 0677. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free lines by country.*

## Notes for Elijah

- **The 1,000 calorie line.** I kept it once, framed as the mistake it was,
  because the story needs it. Eating disorder safe-messaging guidance (Beat's
  media guidelines, for one) says to avoid specific low calorie figures, since a
  vulnerable reader can take one as a target. If you'd rather soften it,
  replace that sentence with: "That put me far below what a growing kid needs."
  I left out the 1,500 figure and any weights for the same reason.
- **Where the BMR number came from.** The draft says "I found a number for what
  I burned." If MyFitnessPal itself gave it to you, or a website, say so. Don't
  blame MFP for something it didn't do.
- **The opening scene.** "Calories remaining" at the bottom of the diary is
  real MFP layout, but the details (closing the app at night, going to bed
  hungry) are mine. Swap in a real moment if you have one. A specific memory
  will beat anything I wrote.
- **"What changed" is vague on purpose.** I don't know what actually helped:
  time, a person, therapy, a different way of eating. Put the real thing in.
  Keep it short and keep it free of tips.
- **The run of days.** The App Store listing still says there are no streaks.
  The blog describes the forgiving run behind `release.celebrations`. Check it
  is on in prod before this goes up, or cut that paragraph.
- **Pattern detection** is described as a goal, not a feature. Today the app has
  the low goal note, the big day message, and the "If eating feels hard right
  now, talk to someone" link on Day close. Keep the wording honest about what
  ships.
- **The line to fourteen-year-olds.** It's the most honest thing in the piece,
  but it is a business decision too (the App Store rating is 12+). Keep it if
  you agree with it.
- **Resources.** The numbers match the app's Find support list
  (`SupportDirectory.kt`, checked 2026-10-27). NEDA's helpline closed in 2023,
  which is why the US line is ANAD and the Alliance. Recheck the numbers before
